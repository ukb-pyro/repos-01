Your question cuts right to something the transformer architects do quietly acknowledge but rarely foreground: the human brain might be less like a database and more like a compressed model — and if so, DeepSeek's efficiency story is actually an argument about biological memory, not just AI scaling.

Let me build the analogy out visually.

Your intuition is essentially correct, and it cuts deeper than most AI discourse acknowledges. Here's the argument:

Human memory is not a tape recorder. It's a lossy encoder followed by a reconstructive decoder. What survives encoding isn't the episode — it's the statistical schema that the episode contributed to. You can't replay your third birthday, but you have very compressed beliefs about what birthdays are, what it felt like to be small, what cake tastes like. That's the latent space. The "remembering" is a decode operation — reconstruction from schema, not playback from storage.

The baffling compute question.

What stunned OpenAI's architects wasn't that language modeling was hard — it was how much scale was needed before emergent capabilities appeared. The assumption was that intelligence scales linearly with data. What they found was that the latent space has to become geometrically rich enough for complex attractors to form. Human brains had ~100 million years of biological "pretraining" and 20 years of developmental fine-tuning before they got there. GPT-4 needed ~$100M of compute to get there by brute force.

The DeepSeek insight — and your biological parallel.

DeepSeek didn't start from scratch. It initialized on top of a latent space already shaped by LLaMA and OpenAI outputs. It inherited the eigenmode. The marginal compute to refine an existing compressed representation is vastly lower than building it from random initialization. This is exactly what happens in human cultural transmission — children don't reconstruct physics from first principles, they inherit the latent structure of human knowledge encoded in language, ritual, and example. Civilization is a knowledge distillation pipeline across generations.

The real architectural question this raises.

If weights are the eigenmode of training data rather than a copy of it, then the qualitative character of what goes into training matters enormously. Human memory doesn't just compress random sensory input — the encoding is emotionally gated (the M layer from your document's TMVES model). High-valence events get deeper encoding. Fear, love, surprise all act as learning rate schedulers. LLMs have no such gate — they compress everything at equal weight unless you engineer it in. That asymmetry might explain why human cognition feels so selective in a way that raw scale doesn't replicate.

The question of whether consciousness emerges from sufficient latent-space complexity is the next step in this argument — and nobody has a clean answer yet.
